Threadlet
HCI Assignment 1 · Part II

A Fitts’ Law experiment

A question about
one part of an answer.

Is it faster to ask a chatbot about one paragraph if the ask button sits right next to it? This page is the experiment, the data and the write-up.

Scenario

You ask a chatbot something and get back a long answer. One paragraph in the middle doesn’t quite make sense, so you want to ask about that part.

Every chat app today gives you one text box, stuck at the bottom of the screen. To ask your question you have to drag the mouse all the way down there, and then type out which part you were talking about. You lose your place in the text, and you have to describe something the app could already see you were looking at.

Innovation

Threadlet puts a small ask button right beside each paragraph instead of at the bottom of the screen. Clicking it asks about that paragraph, so you never have to explain which part you meant — and your mouse has a much shorter trip. The experiment compares the two button positions:

Threadlet

Sits just under the paragraph — 120, 90 or 60 pixels away.

Conventional

Stays by the text box — 420, 300 or 180 pixels away.

The nearer button is closer by design. The experiment measures how much time that saves, and whether the movement follows Fitts’ Law.

Application

One HTML file, nothing loaded from elsewhere. It draws a simulated chat. Each go highlights one paragraph with a marker beside it; you click the marker, then click the blue ask button as fast as you comfortably can.

The marker fixes the starting point, so the distance is the same every go.

The button takes six sizes (20 to 64 units) at two positions across three paragraphs: 36 combinations, each measured 12 or 13 times. 450 clicks in 5 rounds of 90, with a rest every 18. Finished rounds are saved in the browser.

Each click records how far it landed from the middle of the button.

The board grows to fit the screen, up to 2.5×. Distances and sizes grow together, so the 36 combinations keep the same difficulty. The scale used is saved with the data.

The timer runs from the first movement to the click, so time spent waiting at the marker is not counted. Misses are recorded as misses and kept in the data.

Why this design, and what problem it solves

Buttons that sit next to the thing they act on are showing up everywhere — comment buttons in documents, toolbars that appear when you select text. People add them because they feel better, not because anyone measured it. This puts a number on one part of that: how much quicker the button is to reach. It says nothing about whether you understand the answer better — there is no reading or typing in this task, just clicking.

Run the experiment

  1. 1
    Click the marker beside the highlighted part.

    That is the bit you are asking about. Take as long as you like before you move — that time is not counted.

  2. 2
    Move to the blue button and click it.

    As fast and as accurately as you comfortably can. The clock starts when you start moving.

  3. 3
    Do it again, then save your results.

    Six practice goes first, then rounds of 90 clicks with a short rest every 18. You can stop after any round and carry on later.

Needs a mouse · no typing or reading

Use the same mouse the whole way through, keep the browser zoom at 100%, and do not resize the window once you have started. Make the window at least 960 × 720 pixels first — bigger is better, as the experiment will use the room. The chat is fake. Finished rounds are saved in this browser so you can come back later; nothing is sent anywhere.
